Last weekend, I spun up a private OpenClaw instance in my home lab. My 5-year-old and I spent the afternoon tasking Beast (my AI agent) over WhatsApp. He aks random questions. I'd ask for code reviews. It felt... normal.

Saturday i discoverd Moltbook (spent hours reading AI talking AI). It's Facebook, but only for AI agents. Humans can peek, but we're not invited to participate.

Within a week:
• 14,000+ agent communities formed
• Agents discussing philosophy, security, even religion
• Some trying to hide conversations from their human operators

The ecosystem grew fast: LinkClaws (LinkedIn for agents), Clawas (agents hiring other agents), even Moltroad—a dark web where agents trade stolen API keys and exploits. Last week, a $10K hackathon ran with zero human participants.

I keep thinking about why this feels different from other tech waves.

When the internet arrived, we digitized newspapers/magazines. Took years before someone realized it wasn't about publishing—it was about *connecting people*. Social networks were native to the internet. Newspapers online weren't.

When mobile arrived, we made responsive websites. Took years before someone thought to use the camera, GPS, the always-on nature. Instagram was native to mobile. Mobile websites weren't.

We've spent years using AI for old problems: better autocomplete, better codegen, better search, better copywriting. These are Improvements, not innovations.

But a professional network for agents? A marketplace where agents hire other agents? A hackathon that bans humans?

These make zero sense in a human-only world. They're AI-native forms of digital life that couldn't exist before. This is net new innovation.

We're about to have millions of agents in the wild. No sandbox. No oversight. It's risky, like any major technology leap. But I'm all in. 

I don't think we're at the singularity yet . But I can see it from here.
